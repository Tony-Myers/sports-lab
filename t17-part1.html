Where does the p-value come from? The

Would save us? An honest Bayes factor rarely points to an effect when there is none (about 2% here). Choosing among outcomes, outlier rules and subgroups after seeing the data pushes it up as well (about 40% for the Full hacker). Peeking on its own moves it much less (about 4%). Stopping early is a different issue from searching through outcomes and subgroups: whether it is a problem for Bayes factors depends on the prior and on which property you care about (Hendriksen, de Heide and Grünwald, 2021, discuss this). The main problem is not the statistic. It is choosing the analysis after seeing the data.

Peeking done properly. Looking at data as they arrive is not wrong in itself. Clinical trials do it all the time. What makes it a questionable practice is doing it unplanned, with the usual p < .05 at every look. A planned sequential design fixes the number and timing of looks in advance and uses adjusted thresholds, for example Pocock-type (the same strict threshold at every look) or O’Brien–Fleming-type (very strict early, close to .05 at the end). The false-positive rate then stays where you set it.

How is this calculated?
  • Each simulated study has two groups drawn from the same population, so the true effect is exactly zero. Outcomes are standardised (mean 0, SD 1) and correlated at r; each athlete is female or male with probability 0.5.
  • Every analysis is a Student’s independent-samples t-test, two-sided. A study counts as a “finding” if any analysis the switched-on tricks allow gives p < .05.
  • Outlier removal tries each analysis twice: with all data, and after dropping values more than the cut-off in SDs from their own group’s mean. Subgroups with fewer than 3 per group are skipped.
  • The interval on the meter is a Wilson 95% confidence interval for the proportion of studies.
  • The Bayes factor is the default two-sample JZS Bayes factor (Cauchy prior, scale 0.707), as in JASP. A Bayesian “finding” is BF₁₀ > 3 for any analysis.
  • The null distribution plots show the t distribution with the analysis’s degrees of freedom. The p-value is the area in both tails beyond the observed t.

In real research, and check your understanding

From the literature: sport and exercise science

Small samples, low power, and signs of publication bias

Mesquida and colleagues examined studies published in the Journal of Sports Sciences. They note that an earlier study (Abt et al., 2020) had found a median sample size of 19. Across 89 studies they meta-analysed, they found some indication of publication bias, and the average observed power was low: 53% across significant and non-significant findings, and 61% for significant findings only. They also describe the reporting of sample size justifications as suboptimal.

How it connects: small samples usually have low power. Small samples alone do not raise the false-positive rate of a valid planned test. Each extra outcome, subgroup or peek is another chance for luck, and a study with low power that does reach p < .05 tends to overstate the effect (Part 3).

Mesquida, C., Murphy, J., Lakens, D., & Warne, J. (2023). Publication bias, statistical power and reporting practices in the Journal of Sports Sciences: Potential barriers to replicability. Journal of Sports Sciences, 41(16), 1507–1517. https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2023.2269357 (opens in a new tab)

From the literature: sport and exercise

Almost every hypothesis is supported

Büttner and colleagues checked original research in sport and exercise medicine journals. Of 129 studies that stated a hypothesis, 106 (82.2%) reported that their hypothesis was supported. The authors argue that this is implausibly high, given the small samples typical of the field, and that questionable research practices are a likely reason. A registered report in kinesiology by Twomey and colleagues found a similar positive result rate (about 81%), with trial registration and accessible data rarely reported.

How it connects: if most published studies “find” what they hoped for, ask how many tricks from this page might have been used along the way.

Büttner, F., Toomey, E., McClean, S., Roe, M., & Delahunt, E. (2020). Are questionable research practices facilitating new discoveries in sport and exercise medicine? The proportion of supported hypotheses is implausibly high. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 54(22), 1365–1371. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-101863 (opens in a new tab) · Twomey, R., Yingling, V., Warne, J., et al. (2021). The nature of our literature: A registered report on the positive result rate and reporting practices in kinesiology. Communications in Kinesiology, 1(3). https://doi.org/10.51224/cik.v1i3.43 (opens in a new tab)
