Sampling variability and the sampling distribution

Weekly training hours in a large population of club athletes are skewed: most train 4–10 hours, a few train far more. Take a sample, work out its mean, and repeat many times. The pattern those sample means make is the sampling distribution, and its spread is the standard error. Every p-value and confidence interval in later topics rests on it.

Regression to the mean

A coach tests 60 players’ 20 m sprint, picks the 15 slowest for extra sessions, and retests them. Here there are no extra sessions and no training effect: everyone is just retested. Watch what happens to the slowest 15 anyway.

What to notice

  1. The population is skewed, but with n = 30 the sample means already form a roughly bell-shaped pile. That is the central limit theorem.
  2. Increase n from 10 to 40. The spread of sample means halves: precision improves with the square root of the sample size.
  3. Switch to the median. For this skewed population, sample medians are centred on the population median (a different number from the mean) and have their own spread.
  4. In the second panel the slowest players “improve” with no training at all. Any study that selects athletes for being extreme needs a control group.

How it works

population weekly hours, mean μ ≈ 7.7, SD σ ≈ 3.7 (right-skewed) standard error of the mean = σ / √n regression to the mean: expected retest = mean + r × (first score − mean)

A player who is slow on one test day is, on average, partly slow and partly unlucky. The unlucky part does not repeat, so the group of slowest players moves towards the average on retest. The lower the test–retest correlation, the bigger this effect.
