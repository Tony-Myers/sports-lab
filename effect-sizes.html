Effect sizes

An intervention group sprints 20 m faster on average than a control group. How big is that difference? Here one result is expressed five ways: in seconds, in standard deviations (Cohen’s d), and as three “common language” percentages. The curves show how individual athletes’ times are spread in each group.

What to notice

  1. Keep the means fixed and change the SD. The raw difference stays the same, but d and the percentages change: the same 0.05 s matters more in a squad whose times hardly vary.
  2. Even at d = 0.8, the two groups overlap a great deal. Many control athletes are faster than many intervention athletes.
  3. Change the number of athletes. The effect sizes do not move, but their confidence intervals do. With 10 per group, the interval for d = 1.08 runs from about 0.1 to 2.0.
  4. Cohen offered his labels (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) for behavioural science, and only for when nothing better is known. Whether 0.05 s matters depends on the sport and the athletes: in a 100 m final it is huge.

How it works

Both groups’ times are assumed to be normally distributed with the same SD.

raw difference = control mean − intervention mean d = raw difference / pooled SD U3 = Φ(d) Φ = standard normal cumulative probability overlap = 2 Φ(−|d| / 2) PS = Φ(d / √2) chance a random intervention athlete is faster than a random control athlete
The confidence intervals

The interval for d is exact: it inverts the noncentral t distribution, as packages such as MBESS and effectsize do. The interval for the probability of superiority is obtained by converting the two limits of d. Both assume the normal model above. With small groups, Hedges’ g (d multiplied by a small-sample correction) is slightly smaller than d.

Where this idea comes from

Showing one effect size several ways was popularised by Kristoffer Magnusson’s Interpreting Cohen’s d (opens in a new tab). This version was written independently, in sport units, and adds confidence intervals.
