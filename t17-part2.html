↩ Back to Topic 1. Post-test only, change scores and ANCOVA are three estimators of the same estimand (the average effect of the sled programme on 20 m time). They give different estimates. A pre-registration names the estimator in advance.
Who is included? The stated question is about the effect of being assigned to the programme, which an analysis targets. “As randomised” keeps everyone in their assigned group, but with complete cases the four athletes without a post-test still drop out, so it is not a full ITT analysis (the pre-registered analysis uses 17 sled and 19 control athletes). “” keeps only athletes who attended at least 10 of 12 sessions. That changes the question: it is about athletes who attended most sessions, and it estimates the effect of doing the programme only under strong assumptions. In this simulated squad, attendance was not linked to ability, but it did set the dose (each session adds to the effect), and athletes who attended less were more likely to miss the post-test. So per-protocol and complete-case analyses both tilt the target towards athletes who did more of the programme. The shift is small here, from 0.055 s to about 0.057 s, but it is a different question.

Look closer: athlete P30

P30 (control group) ran 3.07 s, 3.56 s and 3.03 s at the post-test. The middle trial is half a second slower than the others: the timing gate triggered late. “Best of 3” and “mean of the best 2” ignore that trial. “Mean of 3” includes it, so P30’s post-test becomes 3.22 s instead of 3.03 s.

For the 72 paths using the mean of trials, omitting the documented faulty trial makes P30’s post-test mean 3.05 s. Recomputing those paths changes estimates by up to 0.0124 s and changes the p < .05 classification in 14 paths. A small numerical change can cross a threshold; it does not necessarily change the practical conclusion. The pre-registration allowed exclusions only for a documented measurement error, and this is one: an analyst who spotted it in advance could legitimately drop that trial.

The published study

Northbridge Sled Study (mock journal abstract). “Sled training improved 20 m sprint time by 0.102 s (95% CI 0.007 to 0.198, p = .036). Groups were compared with a t-test.”

How is this calculated?
  • Outcome: each athlete’s three 20 m trials at each test are summarised as the best (fastest), the mean of all three, or the mean of the best two.
  • Who is included: “as randomised” keeps everyone in their assigned group; per-protocol keeps only athletes who attended at least 10 of 12 sessions, in both groups.
  • Missing post-tests: complete cases drops athletes without a post-test; baseline carried forward assumes they did not change at all. The US National Research Council (2010, Recommendation 10) advises that single imputation methods such as baseline carried forward should not be the primary approach unless their assumptions are scientifically justified. It is included because it is still common, not because it is recommended.
  • Outliers: within each group, athletes more than the cut-off in SDs from their group mean are dropped (using change scores when that estimator is chosen, otherwise post-test times).
  • Estimators: post-test only compares post-test means; change scores compare mean changes; ANCOVA regresses post-test time on group and baseline time.
  • Test: equal spread uses Student’s t (or the usual OLS standard error for ANCOVA); unequal spread uses Welch’s t (or a robust HC3 standard error).
  • The effect is control minus sled, so a positive number means the sled group was faster. You would get the same numbers in JASP, jamovi, R or SPSS.

In real research, and check your understanding

From the literature: many analysts, one dataset

29 expert teams, one question, different answers

Silberzahn and colleagues gave 29 teams of analysts the same football dataset and the same question: are referees more likely to give red cards to players with darker skin tone? Twenty teams found a statistically significant positive effect and nine did not. Their estimates ranged from an odds ratio of 0.89 (slightly lower odds of a red card) to 2.93 (nearly three times the odds).

Different results can arise without misconduct. The teams made different, defensible choices about covariates, models and which data to include.

How it connects: analysis roulette is the same experiment with one analyst. Real experts, working honestly, show the same spread.
