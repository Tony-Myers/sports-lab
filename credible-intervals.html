HDI and ETI

Two hamstring injuries in a squad of 30 players: what is the injury risk? The posterior for a rare event is skewed, and the two common ways of summarising it as a 95% credible interval give different answers. The highest density interval (HDI) holds the most plausible values; the equal-tailed interval (ETI) cuts the same probability from each tail.

What to notice

  1. With 2 of 30 injured, the HDI starts lower and is narrower than the ETI. The ETI excludes some low risks that are more plausible than values it includes near its upper end.
  2. Choose 15 of 30. The posterior is symmetric and the two intervals are the same.
  3. Choose 0 of 25. The most plausible risk is 0, so the HDI starts at 0. The ETI cannot include 0, because it always leaves some probability below its lower limit.
  4. At each end of the HDI the curve is at the same height (the dotted line). That is what “highest density” means.
  5. Both are valid 95% credible intervals. Say which one you report, especially for skewed quantities such as risks, rates and standard deviations.

How it works

With a Beta(a, b) prior and k injured players out of n, the posterior for the injury risk is Beta(a + k, b + n − k). Both intervals are calculated exactly from this distribution.

ETI = the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles (for 95%) HDI = the narrowest interval that holds 95% of the posterior; every value inside is more plausible than every value outside
Which should I report?

Software differs: brms and rstanarm, for example, report ETIs by default, whereas Kruschke’s ROPE approach uses the HDI. The HDI is not invariant to transformations (the HDI for a risk, converted to odds, is not the HDI for the odds), whereas the ETI is. For a roughly symmetric posterior the choice makes no practical difference.
