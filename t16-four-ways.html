One question, four ways to answer it

The Northbridge University Performance Squad runs a planned study: 6 weeks of resisted-sled training against usual training, with 20 m sprint performance as the one outcome. The analysis is fixed in advance and the results are in.

What can you conclude? That depends on which question you ask of the data. Statistics offers four common ways to ask it, and they do not mean the same thing.

A little history: where the four approaches came from

Ronald Fisher developed significance testing in the 1920s; his Statistical Methods for Research Workers appeared in 1925. Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson proposed hypothesis testing as a decision procedure in 1933, and Fisher disagreed with them strongly for the rest of his life. The mixture taught in most textbooks, null hypothesis significance testing (NHST), combined their ideas later; as Gigerenzer (2004) points out, neither side would have endorsed it. Bayesian reasoning is older still (Thomas Bayes’s essay was published in 1763), but it became practical for everyday analyses only with modern computing.

About 8 minutes

The four approaches

Read each card with the sled study in mind. Each one answers a different question, needs different decisions before the data arrive, and can be misread in its own way.

Fisher: evidence against the null

The question: how compatible are these data with a model in which sled training has no effect?

Decide before the data: the null model and the test (here a two-sample t-test). No alternative hypothesis and no power calculation are needed.

You get: an exact p-value, the probability of a result at least this extreme if the null model were true.

Read it as: graded evidence against the null, weighed with the effect estimate, the design and other studies. A surprising result is a reason to look again, ideally by repeating the study.

It cannot tell you: the probability that the null is true, how big or important the effect is, or that there is no effect when p is large.

You met this in Topic 14.

Neyman–Pearson: a rule for deciding

The question: which action should I take, so that over many studies I am rarely wrong?

Decide before the data: two hypotheses (no effect, and an effect worth detecting), α (the false-positive rate you will accept) and a sample size that gives enough power (1 − β).

You get: a decision: reject the null, or accept it, which means acting as if it were true. “Accept” is a decision about what to do, not a belief that the null is true and not proof of no effect.

Read it as: a rule for behaviour with known long-run error rates. Only the side of the line matters: p = .001 and p = .04 lead to the same decision.

It cannot tell you: how strong the evidence is in this particular study, or the probability that this decision is right. The error rates hold only if the whole procedure, including the analysis, was fixed in advance and its assumptions are met. Topic 17 shows what happens when it is not.

You met this in Topic 15.

NHST: the everyday mixture

The question: is p below .05?

Decide before the data: usually only α = .05. The alternative hypothesis and the power are often left out.

You get: the exact p (Fisher’s idea) plus a “significant” or “not significant” label (Neyman–Pearson’s idea).

Read it as: what most papers report, so you need to recognise it. Mixing an evidence measure with a decision rule invites over-reading.

It cannot tell you: anything the other two cannot, and it adds its own traps: treating p = .049 and p = .051 as different findings, and reading “not significant” as “no effect”.

You met this in Topics 14 and 15.

Bayesian estimation: what to believe now

The question: given the data and what we knew before, which effect sizes are plausible?

Decide before the data: a prior for the effect and a model for the data.

You get: a posterior distribution, a 95% credible interval and direct probabilities, such as the probability that the effect is bigger than a worthwhile threshold. This page uses d = 0.2 as an illustration; the right threshold depends on the sport and the outcome (Topic 10).

Read it as: what to believe about the effect after this study, stated as probabilities.

It cannot tell you: anything free of the prior. With little data the prior matters a lot, and tuning a prior to obtain a preferred conclusion undermines inference. Report later changes as sensitivity checks. Comparing “no effect” with “an effect” is the job of Bayes factors (Topic 19).

From the literature: professional guidance

The American Statistical Association’s statement on p-values

In 2016 the American Statistical Association published six principles on p-values, its first statement of this kind. Among them: “P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true”; “Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold”; and “A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.”

How it connects: the first principle is the misreading in the prediction at the top of this page; the second is the main weakness of NHST; the third is why the Bayesian row reports the probability of exceeding the d = 0.2 threshold.

Wasserstein, R. L., & Lazar, N. A. (2016). The ASA’s statement on p-values: Context, process, and purpose. The American Statistician, 70(2), 129–133. https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2016.1154108 (opens in a new tab)
