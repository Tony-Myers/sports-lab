Now the sled programme may or may not have a real effect. A small lab runs the original study (20 athletes per group) with the Full hacker tricks from Part 1: two outcomes, outlier removal, top-ups and a split by sex.

Publication rule (different from Part 1): a study is published only if it finds p < .05 (two-sided) and the sled group did better. In Part 1 a result in either direction counted as a finding; here a result in the “wrong” direction goes in the file drawer, because nobody publishes “sled training makes you slower”. If sled training does nothing, an honest study is publishable 2.5% of the time, half of the 5%.

Why a credible interval here, and not a Bayes factor?

Each replication shows a Bayesian 95% : the range of effect sizes that are plausible after combining the data with a prior. Two assumptions sit behind it: the usual two-sample model (normally distributed scores with equal spread in both groups), which gives the likelihood, and the prior, centred on no effect with SD 0.5 (most training effects between d = −1 and 1). Different, equally reasonable priors give somewhat different intervals; in “What should we believe now?” below you can compare priors, or build your own.

A Bayes factor answers a different question: which of two models predicted the data better, “no effect” or an alternative with its own prior. Evidence favouring “no effect” can occur even when a small effect exists. With a true effect of d = 0.2 and 100 per group, about 45% of replications give BF₀₁ > 3 (at least three times more support for the no-effect model than this alternative) with JASP’s default prior. That is not a mistake in the calculation. It comes from the prior (which spreads its weight over larger effects), the sample size and ordinary sampling variation together. Topic 19 looks at this in detail.

What should we believe now?

A p-value cannot tell you how big the effect probably is. A posterior can, as long as you are honest about the prior and about how the data were produced.

Build your own prior

Before looking at these data, what did you believe about the effect of sled training on sprint performance (d, positive = sled group better)? Use your own knowledge, or a published study.

Where does your prior come from?

Trusting a study less widens the prior (its SD is divided by the square root of the trust). Reasons to trust it less: publication bias (see “Two literatures” below), a different population or outcome, or a different question altogether.

The example values come from a real meta-analysis of resisted sled training: a pooled improvement in full-sprint performance of SMD 0.38 (95% CI 0.10 to 0.67). Check what it estimated before trusting it: most of that is improvement from pre-test to post-test within sled groups, and the authors report that, compared with control groups, the improvement was not significant. Our question is sled versus usual training, so this prior answers a different question and deserves less trust.

The plot shows the three ingredients from Topic 13: the prior (what you believed before), the likelihood (what these data alone say) and the posterior (the two combined). Change the prior: with lots of data the posterior hardly moves; with one small study, the prior matters much more. Bayes factors use the same ingredients to compare “no effect” with an alternative: that is Topic 19.

Two literatures

200 labs each run one honest study (10 to 50 athletes per group). A traditional journal always publishes findings and only sometimes publishes the rest. A journal accepts studies before the results exist, so it publishes all 200.

This is an idealised demonstration of removing selection on results. Real Registered Reports remove that filter, but other filters (which studies are started, submitted or completed) can remain.

How is this calculated?
  • Effect size: Cohen’s d = (sled mean − control mean) ÷ pooled SD, oriented so that positive means the sled group did better. Its approximate standard error is √((n₁ + n₂)/(n₁n₂) + d²/(2(n₁ + n₂))), and an approximate 95% confidence interval is d ± 1.96 × SE. This approximation is less reliable with small samples; intervals after selective analysis do not retain nominal 95% coverage.
  • Replications test the published outcome in the published group (everyone, women or men), with no outlier removal and no peeking. The simulation gives every outcome and both sexes the same true effect.
  • Posterior and credible interval: the likelihood for the true effect δ comes from each study’s t statistic, which follows a noncentral t distribution with noncentrality δ√(n₁n₂/(n₁ + n₂)). The posterior is prior × likelihood, computed on a fine grid, and the 95% credible interval runs from its 2.5th to its 97.5th percentile. Combining replications multiplies their likelihoods, assuming they share one true effect.
  • Meta-analysis: fixed-effect, weighting each published study by 1/SE².
  • Funnel plots: each point is one study (d against its SE, with small SE at the top). The shaded triangle is an approximate non-significance region (|d| < 1.96 × SE); small-sample t-test boundaries differ.

Reflect, and check your understanding

Back to the coach’s post from Part 1.

“After 6 weeks of sled training our women jumped significantly higher (p = .03).”

Three questions a sceptic should ask: How many outcomes (and subgroups) were tested? Was the analysis pre-registered? Are the data and code available?

How convinced are you now?

Five key ideas

  1. A p-value only keeps its promised error rate if the analysis was chosen before seeing the data.
  2. Each flexible choice (outcomes, outliers, peeking, subgroups) is a fork. Lots of forks make a false finding likely. Peeking is fine when it is planned, with adjusted thresholds.
  3. The same data can give very different answers. Showing the whole multiverse, or pre-registering one path, keeps things honest.
  4. = same data + same code → same result (requires access to the same data and computational procedures; sharing them enables others to check). = new data → consistent result (needs new, well-powered studies).
  5. Publication bias inflates effects across a whole literature. Pre-registration and Registered Reports fix the question, estimand and analysis in advance (back to Topic 1).
From the literature: psychology

Replicating 100 studies

The Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 published psychology studies as closely as they could. Of the original studies, 97% had statistically significant results. Of the replications, 36% did. Only 47% of the original effect sizes fell inside the replication’s 95% confidence interval.

How it connects: this is the pattern from the replications step: originals that passed a significance filter, then bigger, honest replications finding smaller effects.

Open Science Collaboration. (2015). Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science. Science, 349(6251), aac4716. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716 (opens in a new tab)

From the literature: sport and exercise science

Replicating sport and exercise science

Murphy and colleagues repeated 25 studies from sport and exercise science. Only 7 of the 25 (28%) met all three of their criteria for a successful replication. These selected studies are not a representative estimate for the whole field.

How it connects: your own field has the same problem, and the same remedies apply: larger, pre-registered replications.

Murphy, J., Caldwell, A. R., Mesquida, C., et al. (2025). Estimating the replicability of sports and exercise science research. Sports Medicine, 55, 2659–2679. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02201-w (opens in a new tab)

From the literature: Registered Reports

96% versus 44%

Scheel, Schijen and Lakens compared 152 standard psychology papers with 71 Registered Reports. In the standard papers, 96% of the first hypotheses tested were supported. In the Registered Reports, accepted before the results were known, 44% were.

How it connects: this real-journal comparison is consistent with the selection illustrated in “Two literatures”. It is observational: other differences between the studies may also contribute.

Scheel, A. M., Schijen, M. R. M. J., & Lakens, D. (2021). An excess of positive results: Comparing the standard psychology literature with Registered Reports. Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 4(2). https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211007467 (opens in a new tab)
